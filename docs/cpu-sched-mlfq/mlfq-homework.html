Q1

Run a few randomly-generated problems with just two jobs and two queues; compute the MLFQ execution trace for each. Limit the length of each job and turn off I/Os.

Answer

With no I/O and two queues (10 ms slice, allotment of one slice), every job gets one slice in Q1 in arrival order; a job longer than 10 ms then drops to Q0, where the jobs share the CPU in round robin with 10 ms slices.

  • Seed 2 (19 ms, 2 ms): J0 runs 0–10 in Q1 and drops; J1 runs 10–12 in Q1 and finishes; J0 finishes in Q0 at 21. Turnaround 21 and 12.
  • Seed 5 (19 ms, 24 ms): both use their Q1 slice (0–10, 10–20), then Q0 round robin: J0 20–29 (done), J1 29–43.
  • Seed 6 (24 ms, 15 ms): Q1: J0 0–10, J1 10–20; Q0: J0 20–30, J1 30–35 (done), J0 35–39.

Try more seeds: -s 7, -s 9, -s 10. Work the trace out first, then add -c.

Q2

How would you run the scheduler to reproduce each of the examples in the chapter?

Answer

Use -l start,run,ioFreq:… to pin the jobs, -q 10 for the book's 10 ms slices, and -S for figures drawn under the old rules 4a/4b (8.4, 8.5, 8.6 left). The I/O lengths (-i) are my choice; the chapter only draws the shapes.

  • 8.2: the long job steps down Q2 → Q1 → Q0 after 10 ms at each level.
  • 8.3: short job B arrives at 100 on top, finishes in 20 ms (turnaround 20) while A waits in Q0: MLFQ approximates SJF.
  • 8.4: the interactive job does I/O every 1 ms, never uses up its allotment, and stays in Q2.
  • 8.5: without a boost, A gets nothing while B and C keep Q2 busy (100–200); with -B 50, A is lifted back and runs at 100–110.
  • 8.6: with -S, the job doing I/O every 9 ms stays in Q2 and takes 90% of the CPU; without -S (Rule 4) it sinks and shares.
  • 8.7: -Q 10,20,40 gives each lower queue a longer slice.
Q3

How would you configure the scheduler parameters to behave just like a round-robin scheduler?

Answer

Make sure no job ever changes queue. Either:

  • One queue: -n 1 -q 10. Rules 1, 3, 4 and 5 have nothing to do, and rule 2 is plain round robin with a 10 ms slice.
  • An allotment too big to use up: -a 100 (100 slices) with no boost. Every job stays in the top queue.

Both match the ch.7 simulator: python3 ../cpu-sched/scheduler.py -p RR -q 10 -l 30,30,30 -c also gives avg response 10 and turnaround 80.

Q4

Craft a workload with two jobs and scheduler parameters so that one job takes advantage of the older Rules 4a and 4b (-S) to game the scheduler and obtain 99% of the CPU over a particular time interval.

Answer

Use a slice of 100 ms and make job 1 issue a 1 ms I/O after every 99 ms of CPU: it gives up the CPU just before its slice ends. With -S (old rule 4b), each I/O resets its allotment, so it stays in the top queue forever.

  • From t = 100 to 700, job 1 gets 99% of the CPU; job 0 only runs during each 1 ms I/O.
  • Drop -S (new Rule 4): job 1 uses up its allotment despite the I/Os, sinks, and the two share 50/50.
Q5

With a 10 ms quantum in the highest queue, how often would you have to boost (-B) to guarantee that a single long-running, potentially-starving job gets at least 5% of the CPU?

Answer

After each boost the long job is back in the top queue, where it runs one 10 ms slice (allotment 1) before it drops again. So it gets 10 ms per boost period B:

10 / B ≥ 5% → B ≤ 200 ms

  • Two interactive jobs (-S, I/O every 1 ms) keep the top queue busy, so without a boost the long job gets 0%.
  • -B 200: 5.0% over t = 100–900. -B 400: 2.5%.
  • With an allotment of k slices at the top, the bound becomes B ≤ 200·k ms.
Q6

Which end of a queue should a job that just finished I/O go to? Play with the -I flag and see its effect.

Answer

By default a job that finishes I/O goes to the back of its queue. With -I it goes to the front, and since the simulator picks the head of the highest queue every tick, it runs right away and cuts into the current job's slice.

  • Workload: one queue, CPU-bound J0 (60 ms) and interactive J1 (30 ms, 1 ms of CPU then 3 ms of I/O).
  • Default: J1 waits behind J0's whole 10 ms slice after every I/O. Turnaround J0 65, J1 162.
  • -I: J1 runs as soon as its I/O is done. J1 drops to 127, J0 rises to 77.

It helps I/O-bound (interactive) jobs at the cost of CPU-bound ones. With several queues and no I/O-heavy job in the same queue, the flag changes nothing.
